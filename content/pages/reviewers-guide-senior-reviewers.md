# Senior Reviewer Instructions

Senior reviewers are responsible for writing the type of review authors traditionally
expect when submitting a manuscript. They should comment on how the work is situated in
the literature, its relevance, and the validity of its claims (that is, its
correctness). Note that one of the goals of RLJ's review process is to avoid
tastemaking and to discourage reviews that focus on the paper the reviewer wishes had
been submitted rather than the paper that was actually submitted. The instructions
below are intended to clarify these expectations and to promote reviews that focus on
the manuscript as submitted. For the wider context, see the
[RLJ Reviewers Guide](/reviewers-guide).

The overall advice to senior reviewers is to focus on answering three questions:

1. Does the work provide evidence supporting the main contributions proposed by the
   authors — are claims matched by evidence? **This includes technical correctness.**
2. Is there an audience for the work?
3. What knowledge gap did the paper address?

The cover page in every submission should be seen almost as a rubric for such an
evaluation: *Is every claimed contribution properly contextualized, and does the work
provide sufficient evidence to support each one of them?*

## Recommended Process

1. Read the cover page, identifying the claimed contributions and assessing whether
   they are accurately and appropriately situated within the literature.
2. Read the manuscript, with a focus on:
   - Identifying the evidence provided for each claimed contribution and evaluating
     whether it sufficiently supports the claim. This includes determining if results,
     whether theoretical or empirical, are correct and scientifically rigorous.
   - Identifying additional claims made in the manuscript that are not listed on the
     cover page and evaluating whether they are adequately supported. Major
     contributions should not be claimed in the main text if they do not appear on the
     cover page.
   - Identifying technical errors, unsupported statements, or points of imprecision.
   - Assessing how the manuscript positions itself relative to prior work.
3. Consider whether:
   - The results are likely to be of interest to a subset of RLJ readers (or RLC
     attendees) beyond the authors themselves; that is, there should be at least five
     people (other than the authors) who read RLJ (or attend RLC) and who would be
     interested in reading the paper.
   - The presentation is professional, clear, well-organized, and written with a
     reinforcement learning audience in mind.
4. Reflect on your overall assessment of the work and how you would answer the review
   questions below.
5. Draft your responses to the questions below and enter them in OpenReview.

## Additional Considerations

- If the paper contains both theoretical and empirical contributions, assess the
  relationship between them. Does the theory provide insight into, justify, or analyze
  the proposed algorithm? Does it apply to the algorithm as implemented and evaluated
  experimentally? If there is a disconnect, is it clearly acknowledged and explained?
- Evaluate claims in proportion to the evidence provided. Broad claims, such as
  state-of-the-art performance across a class of environments, require correspondingly
  broad and convincing empirical support.
- Pay close attention to the references. The reference list is part of the manuscript
  and should be reviewed accordingly. Verify that all cited papers exist, that the
  listed authors are correct, and that publication venues are accurately reported. This
  is particularly important when generative AI tools are used, which can introduce
  citation errors and fabricated references.

## Organize Your Review as Follows

Unlike technical reviewers, senior reviewers are not asked to complete a series of
structured questions. Instead, they are provided with two text boxes: one to explicitly
discuss the paper's claimed contributions, and another to provide a traditional review.

### Claimed contributions box

Reviewers are expected to discuss where the evidence to support each claimed
contribution is in the paper, and whether the provided evidence is sufficient to match
the claims. The [technical reviewer instructions](/reviewers-guide/technical-reviewers)
explicitly require them to answer the questions below. We encourage senior reviewers to
address the same points **for each contribution**. Editors are expected to check every
review for this.

- State the claimed contribution in your own words.
- State what evidence in the paper was provided (or intended) as support for the claim.
- State whether the evidence was sufficient to establish the claim. This should be a
  short statement of yes / no / partially.
- Explain why the provided evidence was or was not sufficient to establish the claim.
  See [Supporting Claims](/reviewers-guide/supporting-claims) for a discussion of what does and
  does not constitute sufficient support for claims.

### General review

Although the claimed contributions should be used as a rubric that will often decide on
the acceptance or rejection of the paper, a comprehensive review will also talk about
the manuscript's presentation, scholarship, and more, including a statement on whether
the manuscript would be of interest to five or more readers of RLJ. Comment on the
polish and presentation of the paper — see
[Polished and Well Written](/reviewers-guide/polished) — or list any other errors you found or
comments you have.

Inspired by [Sutton's guide for writing good reviews](https://www.dropbox.com/scl/fi/4sfm8x3qlm3xp05xs0cdg/review-advice.rtf?rlkey=t2dvidfscp87w83us8tk0gn57&dl=0), we recommend that reviewers
structure their reviews as follows:

- Summarize what the paper claims to do/contribute and what knowledge gap it addresses.
  Be positive and generous (assuming the paper achieves what it sets out to do).
- Clearly state your recommendation (accept or reject) with one or two key reasons for
  this choice.
- Provide supporting arguments for the reasons for the decision.
- Provide additional feedback to improve the paper. Make it clear that these points are
  here to help, not necessarily part of your decision-making.

## What Not to Do

- Do **not** suggest that a different set of claims would have been more interesting,
  impactful, or worthy of publication. If you believe a claimed contribution is not
  supported by the evidence provided, it is appropriate to explain what claim you
  believe the evidence does support. However, this should be done to clarify the scope
  of the conclusions that can be drawn from the paper, not to redefine the paper around
  a contribution you would have preferred to see.
- Do **not** suggest that the authors should have compared to other work published
  within the last six months.
- Do **not** request additional results unless those are required to support the
  contributions already claimed.
